Challenge 01 · Historical spatial epidemiology Medium

The Ghost Map

London, September 1854. Cholera is spreading through Soho. You have household death counts and public pump locations—but no confirmed source. Can you use QGIS to identify the most likely contaminated pump?

📍 Point analysis◉ Nearest neighbour⬡ Voronoi polygons▦ Historical dataQGIS · Vector

John Snow's 1855 map of deaths and public pumps. Historical image via Wikimedia Commons; consult the original publication in the references below.

🕵️ Your mission

John Snow investigated whether cholera was transmitted through contaminated drinking water at a time when the miasma theory was widely held. Snow's investigation combined geographic distributions with interviews and evidence about actual water use.

Challenge question: Which public pump is most strongly implicated by the mapped household deaths, and how convincing is the spatial evidence?

Do not assume a pump is the cause simply because it has deaths nearby. Your task is to build, test and critically discuss a spatial hypothesis.

✅ Required tasks

  1. Georeference the original map. Load the unreferenced JPG in the QGIS Georeferencer and use the supplied modern basemap to create at least six well-spaced ground control points (GCPs) on recognisable street junctions. Start with an affine / Polynomial 1 transform, use EPSG:27700, report residual errors, and export a newly georeferenced raster. Remember that roads have changed since 1854.
  2. Prepare the data. Load the historical cholera deaths-by-building points and public pump locations into QGIS. Inspect the deaths attribute and use a projected coordinate reference system with distances in metres.
  3. Reconstruct the outbreak map. Symbolise building locations so larger death counts are visually distinguishable, label the pumps and produce a legible overview of Soho.
  4. Measure proximity. Calculate the straight-line distance from each death location to its nearest pump; calculate the distance to the Broad Street pump separately. Use the deaths field as a weight for summaries rather than treating each building as one death.
  5. Construct pump catchments. Create Voronoi (Thiessen) polygons for pumps. Count the number of deaths assigned to each pump's nearest-area polygon and calculate the share associated with each pump.
  6. Test a hypothesis. Compare at least two candidate pumps using total deaths in their catchments and the distribution of death-weighted distances. State whether the Broad Street pump is your strongest candidate.
  7. Communicate uncertainty. Explain why nearest-pump access does not imply which pump residents actually used, why a concentration of deaths is not a mortality rate, and what additional evidence you would need.

📋 Deliverables & marking rubric

  • 25 points — Georeferencing: good control-point placement, documented transformation, acceptable alignment and an honest discussion of map distortion.
  • 30 points — Spatial analysis: valid CRS, correctly calculated nearest-pump assignment and death-weighted totals.
  • 20 points — Catchment comparison: correctly constructed Voronoi polygons and meaningful comparison of candidates.
  • 15 points — Cartography: a map with readable symbols, pump labels, legend, north arrow or orientation, scale and source acknowledgement.
  • 10 points — Scientific interpretation: identify a likely pump, communicate limitations and propose confirmatory evidence.
Pass condition: Deliver a QGIS project, a final map (PDF or PNG), a table showing deaths assigned to each of the available pumps, and a 150–250-word conclusion. The scoring is a self-assessment rubric, not automated grading.

💡 Progressive hints

Attempt the investigation before opening these.

Hint 0 · Aligning the 1855 image

Unzip the pack. Add the GeoPackage to QGIS, then load john_snow_1855_unreferenced.jpg in Layer → Georeferencer (menu location varies by QGIS version). Place GCPs at historic street junctions that are identifiable on the modern OS basemap. Use at least six spread around the image, check residual errors, and export the georeferenced GeoTIFF. The JPG is also embedded as an attachment in the GeoPackage, but QGIS Georeferencer requires the standalone image supplied in the ZIP.

Hint 1 · Which projection?

Use a suitable projected CRS, such as British National Grid (EPSG:27700), for metre-based distances. Reproject layers rather than merely changing the project display CRS. Confirm data coordinates and units from the source documentation.

Hint 2 · How to calculate the nearest pump?

Try Processing Toolbox → Distance to nearest hub (points) or Join attributes by nearest. Make sure the pump identifier is carried into the death layer. GeoDa's building dataset includes derived fields that can be used as a check after you've attempted the calculation.

Hint 3 · How should the catchments work?

Use Processing Toolbox → Voronoi polygons on the pump layer. Use Join attributes by location (summary) or a spatial intersection to sum the deaths field within each polygon. Handle locations exactly on polygon boundaries consistently.

Hint 4 · What might a persuasive answer look like?

Consider whether the pump with the largest assigned number of deaths also has a tighter concentration of deaths. Beware of differing catchment areas and population sizes: counts alone do not demonstrate elevated risk.

🔬 Optional research extension · Hard

Revisit the study by Shiode et al. (2015): death counts alone are not mortality rates because the population at risk varies across homes and streets. Investigate the richer historical data they reconstruct, and propose how you would normalise deaths by residential population or analyse the outbreak over time.

Alternatively, follow Caplan, Kennedy and Neudecker (2020) and design a transparent risk-factor analysis that compares proximity to pumps with other environmental explanations. You do not need to reproduce their specialised Risk Terrain Modeling software.
